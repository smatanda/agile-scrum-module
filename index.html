Iterative

Deliver in short cycles

Sprints are 1–4 weeks. Each sprint produces a working, potentially shippable increment. No waiting until the end.

Collaborative

One team, not silos

PM, BA, and Dev share accountability. Ceremonies are not reporting events — they are coordination mechanisms.

Adaptive

Improve every sprint

The retrospective is a built-in improvement engine. Every sprint the team reflects and commits to getting better.

Key artifacts: Product Backlog (full prioritized list) → Sprint Backlog (committed work for this sprint) → Increment (working output delivered at sprint end).
02
Sprint Planning
The sprint starts here. The team aligns on what to build and why — then commits to a realistic goal.
⏱ 2–4 hours for a 2-week sprint
PM

Sets the direction

  • Presents sprint priorities
  • Defines the sprint goal
  • Manages stakeholder expectations
  • Ensures backlog is ordered
BA

Brings clarity

  • Walks through user stories
  • Clarifies acceptance criteria
  • Answers scope questions
  • Confirms stories are Dev-ready
DEV

Commits to scope

  • Estimates effort (story points)
  • Flags technical risks early
  • Commits to achievable work
  • Uses velocity to stay honest
Output

Sprint Backlog

The team's committed plan for the sprint. Every item has an owner, an estimate, and clear acceptance criteria. The sprint goal is the north star — if scope needs to flex, the goal should not.

Common pitfall

Overloading the sprint

Loading more than the team can deliver is the most common Sprint Planning mistake. Use velocity data from past sprints to keep commitments honest. A smaller committed sprint beats a bloated one every time.

Remember: Sprint Planning is a negotiation, not an assignment. PM brings the priorities, BA brings the clarity, Dev brings the reality check. All three inputs are required for a healthy sprint commitment.
03
Backlog Refinement
Not an official Scrum ceremony — but the secret weapon of high-performing teams. Skip it and Sprint Planning becomes chaotic.
⏱ ~1 hour per week, mid-sprint
1

PM reprioritizes the backlog

Based on stakeholder feedback, business changes, or new information. Items move up, down, or get removed entirely.

2

BA refines and writes acceptance criteria

User stories get fleshed out with detailed requirements and clear done-criteria. By the end of refinement, the top items in the backlog should be so well understood that Sprint Planning is fast and confident.

3

Dev estimates and flags risks

Stories get estimated before Sprint Planning — not during it. Dev also flags technical dependencies, complexity, or feasibility concerns before they become sprint blockers.

When it works

Sprint Planning takes 30 minutes

The team arrives already knowing what the top items are, what they mean, and roughly how long they'll take. Commitment is fast and confident.

When it's skipped

Sprint Planning takes 4 hours

Stories are vague, estimates are guesses, and the team spends Planning doing what should have been done earlier. Dev and BA go back and forth while everyone else waits.

Goal: By the time Sprint Planning starts, the next sprint's backlog is already ready. BA is the key player here — clear, well-written stories with solid acceptance criteria prevent the majority of downstream rework.
04
The Daily Scrum
A 15-minute team sync — every day, same time, same place. Not a status meeting. A team alignment huddle.
⏱ 15 minutes maximum
01
What did I do yesterday that moved us toward the sprint goal?
02
What will I do today to move us toward the sprint goal?
03
Is anything blocking me or the team?
PM

Listen and capture

Surface organizational blockers and stakeholder dependencies. Your job is to resolve blockers after the meeting — not run the meeting.

BA

Flag clarity gaps

If a Dev is about to build something based on an incomplete requirement, now is the time to catch it — before a day of work goes in the wrong direction.

DEV

Raise blockers early

Technical blockers, scope concerns, dependencies. If a story is bigger than estimated, say so now — not on demo day.

Signs it's working
  • Ends under 15 minutes consistently
  • Blockers resolved same day or next
  • Team talks to each other, not the PM
  • Everyone comes prepared
Signs it's breaking down
  • Regularly runs 30+ minutes
  • PM does most of the talking
  • Vague answers: "working on stuff"
  • People skip or show up late
The golden rule: The team talks to each other — not to the PM. The moment the PM starts directing traffic, the Daily Scrum becomes a status meeting and loses its value. Stay quiet, listen, and act on blockers after the huddle.
05
Sprint Review
The team demonstrates what was built. Stakeholders respond. The backlog gets updated. This is how the team builds trust with the business.
⏱ 1–2 hours per sprint
PM

Frames the business value

Opens the review by connecting what was built to the sprint goal and the broader project objectives. Sets context for stakeholders before the demo begins.

BA

Walks through requirements vs. what was built

Presents the user stories and acceptance criteria, then demonstrates how the increment meets (or raises questions about) the original requirements.

DEV

Demos the working increment

Shows the actual working software — not slides, not mockups. The Product Owner formally accepts or rejects items against their acceptance criteria.

Output

Updated Product Backlog

Stakeholder feedback from the review directly shapes the next sprint. New items get added, priorities shift, and the team leaves with a clearer picture of what to build next.

Not a sign-off meeting

The goal is feedback, not approval

Stakeholders are invited to react, question, and redirect — not just applaud. The most valuable reviews are the ones where stakeholders challenge assumptions and the team learns something new.

Key principle: Everyone presents something. Encourage PM to frame value, BA to bridge requirements, and Dev to demo the product. A review where only Dev presents sends the wrong signal about team ownership.
06
Sprint Retrospective
The team reflects on how it worked together and commits to one or more specific improvements. This is how Scrum teams get better over time.
⏱ 1–1.5 hours per sprint
✓
What went well this sprint?
✗
What didn't work or caused friction?
→
What will we change starting next sprint?
Good retro action

Specific and owned

"BA will share draft user stories with Dev by Wednesday before each refinement session."

Clear owner, clear timing, measurable. You'll know next sprint whether it happened.

Weak retro action

Vague and unowned

"We will improve communication."

No owner. No deadline. No way to know if anything changed. This is the most common retro failure pattern.

1

Set the tone before you start

This is a safe space, not a blame session. The PM models the behavior by sharing something they personally would do differently. Psychological safety is the prerequisite for honest retros.

2

Limit actions to 1–3 per sprint

More than three action items means none of them will get done. Pick the highest-value change and commit to it fully.

3

Follow up at the start of the next retro

Before diving into the new sprint's retrospective, review last sprint's commitments. Did they happen? This builds trust in the process and shows the team that retro actions have real consequences.

Critical reminder: Never skip the retrospective when the sprint was rough. That is exactly when it matters most. The worst retros are the ones that never happen because the team was too busy or too demoralized.
07
How PM, BA & Dev
Work Together
Each role has a distinct lane, but the ceremonies are where those lanes converge. Here's the handoff model.
Ceremony PM BA DEV
Sprint Planning Sets priorities & sprint goal Clarifies stories & criteria Estimates & commits
Backlog Refinement Reprioritizes backlog Writes & refines stories Estimates upcoming work
Daily Scrum Surfaces org blockers Flags requirement gaps Raises technical blockers
Sprint Review Frames business value Presents req vs. built Demos the increment
Retrospective Models vulnerability Highlights process gaps Raises technical friction
The handoff model: PM sets the direction → BA defines the detail → Dev builds and feeds back. But it's not linear — all three roles are in constant dialogue throughout the sprint. The ceremonies are the structure; the relationship is the foundation.
Pitfalls by role
PM pitfall

Changing sprint scope mid-sprint without team agreement. Protect the sprint — if a stakeholder demands something new, it goes into the backlog for the next sprint, not this one.

BA pitfall

Writing requirements in isolation without Dev input. Stories that Dev never reviewed often have hidden technical assumptions that blow up during execution.

DEV pitfall

Going dark until demo day. Daily transparency is non-negotiable — a blocker raised on day 2 can be fixed; the same blocker raised on day 9 kills the sprint.
